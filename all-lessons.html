Lesson 1: The world is a system

Target: Explain how people, AI, MCP tools, software, and hardware contribute different work.

Work in your paper journal or a school-approved document. The times are planning ranges, not timers to wait out. Finish the evidence, then choose another card.

Start here · 2–3 minutes

Sort the tools

Write AI, MCP, Blender, processor, and student designer. Beside each, predict one job. Circle the job you are least sure about.

Student reading · 3–5 minutes

Welcome to Lantern Reach, a fantasy settlement built by one class over ten lessons. We will keep its terrain, tower, bridge, homes, grove, gate, and signs in one changing project. Your ideas matter even when your hands are not on the teacher computer. Each designer keeps a journal that shows a proposal, a prediction, evidence, and a revision.

Blender stores and changes the 3D scene. An AI model can suggest instructions or code. An MCP connection lets an AI application call tools exposed by a server; which tools exist depends on the installed setup. MCP itself is not a 3D artist and does not guarantee correct work. The teacher reviews and runs selected requests. The processor executes instructions, memory holds working data, storage keeps saved files, and the display shows an image. A Chromebook can read a small page or record a plan without running Blender.

A confident AI reply is a claim, not proof that an object exists or has the right dimensions. We compare the actual scene or object report with our acceptance tests. A still image can show appearance but cannot prove hidden dimensions. A paper model can test an algorithm before any software runs. Our first job is to define what success means, rather than ask for something that merely looks impressive.

Trade with a partner: identify two arrows needing explanation; revise independently. Finally propose one fantasy feature within the class limits and give two acceptance tests.

Lesson 2: Decompose before you build

Target: Break the shared world into modules with explicit dependencies and tests.

Work in your paper journal or a school-approved document. The times are planning ranges, not timers to wait out. Finish the evidence, then choose another card.

Start here · 2–3 minutes

Parts or decorations?

List six parts of a fantasy village. Underline the three whose placement could affect a traveler’s route. Explain one choice.

Student reading · 3–5 minutes

A large problem becomes easier to manage when we decompose it into smaller parts. Lantern Reach contains a tower module, a bridge module, a home module, a grove module, and a gate module. Each module has inputs, work, and outputs. A tower may take a position and height as inputs and produce a body and roof as outputs. Decorative wishes become useful when they are attached to a specific module and a test.

Some modules depend on others. The terrain must have a known surface before a tower’s ground contact can be checked. The river corridor must be known before the bridge can be tested. A dependency arrow means “must be ready before,” not simply “looks near.” Independent tasks can be planned at the same time even though only the teacher executes Blender changes.

Abstraction means keeping the details needed for the current decision. A top-down rectangle can stand for a house when we are planning routes; roof shingles do not help that decision. Later, a roof shape may matter. We will preserve stable object names so that a later instruction changes a known module instead of accidentally replacing the whole world. Breaking a world into nouns is only a start. To support a program, each part also needs responsibilities, inputs, and tests.

Individually explain which roof details you omitted from the route plan and why.

Turn in: Five completed cards; dependency diagram; six-step order; abstraction explanation.

Lesson 3: Coordinates make prompts testable

Target: Specify a tower using coordinates, dimensions, and measurable acceptance tests.

Work in your paper journal or a school-approved document. The times are planning ranges, not timers to wait out. Finish the evidence, then choose another card.

Start here · 2–3 minutes

Center or bottom?

A 6-unit-high box is centered at z=1. Find top and bottom. Then find the center needed to place its bottom at ground.

Student reading · 3–5 minutes

Our world uses a shared coordinate system. On the top-down map, x increases east and y increases north. In Blender, z increases upward. Ground level is z=0. A location is an object’s center unless the contract says otherwise. This matters: a box of height 8 centered at z=0 extends from z=-4 to z=4. To rest its bottom on the ground, its center must be at z=4.

The tower body has a 4-by-4 footprint, height 8, and center (-7,4,4). Its x edges are -9 and -5; its y edges are 2 and 6. The roof is a separate cone with height 2 centered at (-7,4,9), so its bottom meets the body’s top at z=8. Units are classroom world units, not a claim about real construction.

A prompt is natural language, so it can contain ambiguities. An algorithm needs ordered, sufficiently precise actions. “Make a tall tower nearby” leaves height, reference point, and distance open. A stronger request names the objects, values, preservation rules, and tests. Predictions must come before inspection: otherwise we may accidentally describe whatever the AI made as if that was our original goal. We also distinguish a desired measurement from a measured result. Until checked, a dimension is a requirement.

Partner acts as a literal executor and marks three possible ambiguities; revise your own request and predict the resulting object count.

Lesson 4: Algorithms across the river

Target: Compare and refine algorithms for a bridge using a stated efficiency measure.

Work in your paper journal or a school-approved document. The times are planning ranges, not timers to wait out. Finish the evidence, then choose another card.

Start here · 2–3 minutes

Bridge coverage

The river extends from x=-2 to 2. A bridge extends from -4 to 4. Find the river width and the bridge coverage on each bank.

Student reading · 3–5 minutes

An algorithm is an ordered method for completing a task. Two algorithms may both produce eight bridge planks but use different representations. Algorithm A writes eight separate create instructions. Algorithm B repeats one instruction eight times while changing x. Repetition can make a procedure easier to revise. It does not automatically mean Blender will create fewer objects or render faster.

Our river corridor runs north–south from x=-2 to x=2. The bridge crosses east–west at y=-4. Eight touching planks are centered at x=-3.5,-2.5,-1.5,-0.5,0.5,1.5,2.5,3.5. Each plank is 1 unit long in x, 3 units wide in y, and 0.4 units high, centered at z=0.2. The bridge extends from x=-4 to x=4, leaving 2 units of bank coverage on each side of the river.

Before calling one algorithm efficient, name your measure. Source lines, human edits, tool calls, object count, and elapsed seconds are different measures. We will count explicit instruction lines and the edits needed when a request changes from eight to ten planks. We will not invent timing data. An off-by-one error happens when a process repeats once too often or too few times. A trace table lets us inspect every position before using a tool.

Required evidence; use partner feedback after your own draft

Compare number of create operations, written instruction lines, and edits needed for ten planks centered on the same crossing. Revise B to use n and start x=-(n-1)/2. State which measure makes B preferable and one measure that stays unchanged.

Turn in: Two algorithms; eight-row trace; three-metric comparison; ten-plank revision.

Lesson 5: Variables and reusable homes

Target: Trace variable values and use parameterized procedures to specify related homes.

Work in your paper journal or a school-approved document. The times are planning ranges, not timers to wait out. Finish the evidence, then choose another card.

Start here · 2–3 minutes

Argument match

For make_home("H2",9,3,5), identify the name, x, y, and height. Predict body center z and roof center z using the reading’s rules.

Student reading · 3–5 minutes

A variable is a name for a value that may change. A parameter is an input named in a procedure definition. An argument is the actual value passed in a call. In make_home("H1",5,3,4), the arguments can provide name, x, y, and body height. The procedure can use height to place the body’s center at height/2 and the roof center at height+1. A change to one procedure can update the shared construction rule.

Our home body has dimensions 3×3×h. Its roof is a cone of depth 2 and radius 2.4. The default home centers in the map are H1=(5,3), H2=(9,3), and H3=(7,7). Height is allowed from 2 to 6 inclusive. A procedure must say what happens to invalid input. Silently turning -2 into a positive value hides an error. We will reject invalid heights and report a reason.

Named inputs make a family of related objects possible, but they also create responsibilities: use the passed height rather than a forgotten constant, use unique names, and check the valid range. Reading code is different from writing a prompt that asks AI to write code. In this lesson you will change actual code text, trace its values, and keep your own version. Teacher execution can supply shared feedback; your journal must still show your individual changes and predictions.

Core design work · 11–16 minutes

Required evidence; use partner feedback after your own draft

Home procedure workshop

Use the code card below. Trace calls H1(5,3,2), H2(9,3,4), H3(7,7,6) in a table with h, body center z, body top, roof center z, roof top. Change the procedure to accept a roof_depth parameter, replacing both related constants. Write two new calls using different valid values. Have a partner trace your calls; compare all five outputs. Record one correction or explain why no correction was needed. Add a list/table holding three home records and explain why that structure is easier to audit than a paragraph.

Turn in: Three-row trace; edited code; two calls and partner traces; three-record collection.

Code card

# Python arithmetic; emit_home records values for a modeling tool.
def make_home(name, x, y, h):
    if h < 2 or h > 6:
        return "REJECT: height must be 2 through 6"
    body_center = h / 2
    roof_center = h + 1
    return (name, x, y, body_center, h, roof_center, 2)

# Return fields: name,x,y,body_center,body_height,roof_center,roof_depth
make_home("H1", 5, 3, 2)
make_home("H2", 9, 3, 4)
make_home("H3", 7, 7, 6)

Lesson 6: Loops and decisions grow the grove

Target: Trace a loop with a conditional and repair an off-by-one error.

Work in your paper journal or a school-approved document. The times are planning ranges, not timers to wait out. Finish the evidence, then choose another card.

Start here · 2–3 minutes

Predict before looping

List the numbers produced by range(4). Then calculate x=-9+2*i for each number.

Student reading · 3–5 minutes

A loop repeats instructions. A conditional chooses which instructions run. They often work together. To plant a grove, we can loop through possible positions and skip any position reserved for a path. In our code card, i takes the values 0,1,2,3,4,5. We calculate x=-9+2*i and use y=-7. When x=-5 we skip planting, leaving a path gap. Five trees remain at x=-9,-7,-3,-1,1.

The condition must be evaluated for each iteration, not once at the end. In Python, range(6) stops before 6. Therefore changing it to range(7) adds another iteration with i=6 and x=3. If the contract allowed only six candidate positions, that is an error even if the picture still looks attractive.

A trace table records i, x, whether the condition is true, and the action. Trace tables expose values that a small screenshot can hide. To test a repaired program, use both a normal case and a boundary case. A repair can fix one input while breaking another. Our tree drawing is deliberately simple: a cylinder trunk and a cone canopy. Planting the grove is a reason to study repetition and choice, not a contest to make the most detailed leaves.

Partner-check every row and write a short explanation of why one test was not enough.

Lesson 7: Debug with evidence

Target: Diagnose faults, isolate a change, and use regression tests on the gate.

Work in your paper journal or a school-approved document. The times are planning ranges, not timers to wait out. Finish the evidence, then choose another card.

Start here · 2–3 minutes

Opening math

Posts are centered at x=4 and 8, each width1. Find their inner edges and the clear gap. Would width2 posts still meet the 2-unit route rule?

Student reading · 3–5 minutes

Debugging is a systematic search for a cause. Start with expected behavior, record actual behavior, form a hypothesis, change one relevant thing, and retest. If you change color, location, dimensions, and code all at once, a successful result may not tell you which change fixed the problem.

The Lantern Reach gate uses two posts and a lintel, so travelers can pass through an opening rather than a solid box. The posts stand at (4,0) and (8,0), each 1 unit wide, 1 unit deep, and 4 units tall, centered at z=2. Their inner x edges are 4.5 and 7.5, giving a clear opening of 3 units. The lintel spans 5 units in x at center (6,0,4.5), height1, so its bottom meets the post tops at z=4.

A screenshot might make the gate look open while a hidden block still fills the gap. Tests therefore include object data and a check for an unwanted blocker. A regression test checks that a previously correct feature stays correct after a change. When fixing the gate, confirm the tower is still height8 and the bridge still has eight planks. A failure report should describe a reproducible case, not blame a person or say merely “it is broken.”

Core design work · 11–16 minutes

Required evidence; use partner feedback after your own draft

Lesson 8: AI claims, learning, and inclusive design

Target: Distinguish training from use and evaluate bias and accessibility with limited evidence.

Work in your paper journal or a school-approved document. The times are planning ranges, not timers to wait out. Finish the evidence, then choose another card.

Start here · 2–3 minutes

Training or use?

Label each: a model’s weights change after learning from examples; the teacher asks an existing model for a tower; a student tests a sign with a classmate. Explain your labels.

Student reading · 3–5 minutes

Using a trained model to produce an answer is often called inference. Training changes a model using data and a learning process. Entering a new prompt normally supplies context for that use; it is not evidence that the underlying model has been retrained. Services may have separate memory or data-use policies, but we cannot infer those policies from one chat. Humans can learn through experience, physical action, goals, and social explanation. Machine learning systems find patterns through a designed training process; a fluent response does not establish human understanding.

Training data can shape what a system handles well. A small classroom dataset is not a diagnosis of our actual AI model. We will use a toy example to reason about possible bias. Suppose a sign classifier is trained on 12 signs: 10 have large print, 2 small print; all are in one language. Its test results are 8 correct of 8 large-print signs, but 1 correct of 4 small-print signs. That suggests a performance gap worth investigating. It does not prove the cause or performance on every language.

Accessible design needs specific criteria and user feedback. Our signs will use words plus symbols, clear contrast, and a route map, rather than color alone. A fantasy world can include many kinds of residents without assigning abilities or jobs by stereotypes. We can improve our design while admitting what we have not tested.

Work in your paper journal or a school-approved document. The times are planning ranges, not timers to wait out. Finish the evidence, then choose another card.

Pass, fail, unknown

Student reading · 3–5 minutes

Integration joins modules into one system. A correct tower and a correct bridge can still form a poor world if no usable route connects them. An acceptance suite checks the whole design against the charter, and regression checks protect earlier successes. Tests need IDs, inputs or observations, expected outcomes, actual outcomes, and a decision. “Not checked” is different from “pass.”

Resource tradeoffs require data. Here is an illustrative benchmark dataset, not a measurement of your teacher computer: version A uses 35 objects and has three usable-build times of 12,14,16 seconds; version B uses 70 objects and has times24,30,36 seconds. The medians are14 and30. We cannot conclude object count alone caused the difference, because other features may differ. We can recommend A when it meets the same acceptance tests and our goal is predictable classroom response.

Our world must keep a route at least2 units wide through the bridge and gate. Tower height must remain8; bridge must retain8 planks; homes must be grounded; grove must keep its path gap. A final beauty render is optional. A saved scene plus a simple viewport image or a labeled paper plan can communicate the design. Lesson 10: Publish the evidence, preserve the world

Target: Present a defensible individual explanation and archive the shared project responsibly.

Work in your paper journal or a school-approved document. The times are planning ranges, not timers to wait out. Finish the evidence, then choose another card.

Start here · 2–3 minutes

Choose the evidence

You may show only three artifacts to explain a bridge algorithm. Choose from final render, trace table, original/revised code, test log, and color palette. Justify your three.

Student reading · 3–5 minutes

A finished computing artifact needs an explanation of its decisions and limits. Lantern Reach is one class project, but each designer can show individual thinking through a before/after example, code, tests, and a justified revision. A polished image alone does not reveal who understood the algorithm or who checked a result.

We will create a release package: the editable scene saved by the teacher, an image or labeled map for viewing, a change log, test results, and credits. A .blend file preserves editable scene data; a PNG image is a view, not an editable 3D replacement. Our original drawings need creator credit, and any external resources would need their source and permission checked. AI assistance should be described honestly, without treating AI output as proof that no outside rights are involved.

Public sharing is a separate decision. Student names, account details, and private classroom records do not belong in a public model or AI prompt. Use a class project title or anonymous design IDs. Before a release, compare the final suite with the charter and label remaining unknowns. If rendering fails, a viewport capture or paper map can still support a strong explanation.
