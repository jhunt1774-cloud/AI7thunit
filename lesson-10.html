Learn this before doing today’s work

How to use this section: read the six explanations,follow the worked examples,then try the two guided questions. Your teacher will discuss them before assigning independent work. Return to the numbered explanation if you need help with a check question.

1. Editable project versus viewing image

A .blend file is Blender’s editable project/scene format. A .png file is an image:a view of the scene,not a replacement for editable geometry. A .csv file stores table-like records,and .txt holds plain text. Keep the editable project for later changes and a viewing artifact for communication. Saving one does not automatically create the others.

2. Separate storage from a release

An archive keeps project files for future work;a release is the selected material shared with an audience. Credentials such as passwords must never enter a release. Private names,grades,and account information stay under school-controlled handling and are excluded from this unit’s shared site. Use anonymous design IDs;the teacher decides approved storage and viewing.

3. Individual evidence explains learning

The class scene is a group product. To show individual understanding,use your own algorithm/code,trace,test records,and explanation of a revision. A trace shows predicted values;a test log compares expected with actual and names its evidence source. A final picture alone does not explain how an individual reasoned or corrected an error.

4. A failed render does not erase the work

A render is a produced image of a scene. Our learning evidence can still include a viewport image,labeled paper map,code,trace,and tests when rendering fails. Label simulated results honestly. A portfolio should explain the method and evidence,not claim a render happened when it did not. No public upload is needed to demonstrate the learning.

5. Credit actual contributions

Credits acknowledge human design,writing,testing,and artwork,plus any used external resources and an honest description of AI assistance. Example:“Class designers proposed features;teacher operated Blender;AI assisted with draft code;students checked dimensions.” Do not claim the AI alone did every task or that AI output automatically settles permission for external material. Keep personal identifiers out of public credits.

6. Defensible claims include limits

A claim is defensible when its evidence supports its scope. “Our measured opening met the2-unit criterion” is narrower than “everyone can always use our world.” A limitation names what is not established and suggests a next test. A strong portfolio connects requirement→method→prediction→evidence→revision and states which results are actual versus simulated.

Connect the idea to our class world

A finished computing artifact needs an explanation of its decisions and limits. Lantern Reach is one class project, but each designer can show individual thinking through a before/after example, code, tests, and a justified revision. A polished image alone does not reveal who understood the algorithm or who checked a result.

We will create a release package: the editable scene saved by the teacher, an image or labeled map for viewing, a change log, test results, and credits. A .blend file preserves editable scene data; a PNG image is a view, not an editable 3D replacement. Our original drawings need creator credit, and any external resources would need their source and permission checked. AI assistance should be described honestly, without treating AI output as proof that no outside rights are involved.

Public sharing is a separate decision. Student names, account details, and private classroom records do not belong in a public model or AI prompt. Use a class project title or anonymous design IDs. Before a release, compare the final suite with the charter and label remaining unknowns. If rendering fails, a viewport capture or paper map can still support a strong explanation. The most valuable final sentence may be a limitation: what evidence would you need before making a stronger claim?

Choice A · suggested 10–18 minutes; move on when evidence is complete

Alternative design defense

Write a counterproposal replacing the bridge loop with explicit calls or changing the grove skip rule. Trace the alternative, compare two metrics, and identify one test it fails or improves. Write a150-word defense that includes evidence supporting the original design as well as your alternative.

Turn in: Alternative algorithm, trace, metric comparison, balanced defense.

Choice B · suggested 10–18 minutes; move on when evidence is complete

Future maintainer challenge

Write a handoff for next year’s class: module inventory with six entries, coordinate convention, naming rules, three known risks, and a five-step safe change procedure. Give it to a partner who must answer four questions without your help: tower height, river bounds, gate minimum, and where tests are stored. Revise missing information.

Turn in: Maintainer guide, four retrieval answers, revised handoff.

Further challenge

Evidence cross-examination

Write three claims you could defend and three claims you cannot yet defend, such as universal accessibility or a guaranteed processing time. For each unsupported claim design the next test, identify needed participants/data, and state a possible disconfirming result.

Turn in: Your supported explanation,trace,or revised plan.
